Income or Distance?

Understanding consumer mobility across Atlanta’s shopping districts

Main finding. Distance is the most consistent predictor of reported activity in the models studied. Wealthier neighborhoods are closer to the two Buckhead malls in the raw data, but income has limited additional explanatory and predictive power once distance and other characteristics are considered.

Abstract

Why do communities travel to different retail districts? Income may shape purchasing power, but distance creates a travel cost, and the two may be correlated because households and retailers choose locations. This study combines neighborhood characteristics, road distances and reported mobility to distinguish descriptive accessibility patterns from conditional associations and model-based predictions. We compare three Atlanta districts over 2024. The central result is a stable negative distance coefficient near −1 across multiple specifications. Income sorting is positive but statistically uncertain. A calibrated spatial share model provides a transparent tool for counterfactual analysis, although a newly evaluated historical-average benchmark predicts the same neighborhoods more accurately. These results are about reported district mobility, not verified purchases or causal effects on neighborhood income.

Reader's roadmap

First we measure opportunities to reach stores; next we observe reported activity; then we link incompatible geographic units. We estimate a gravity model for flow volumes, test its sensitivity, estimate a separate model for relative shares, and finally change one input in a simulation. Each step answers a different question.

1. Research question and data

We compare Lenox Square and Phipps Plaza in Buckhead with Ponce City Market. Do people visit upscale retail districts because of neighborhood income, or simply because those districts are closer?

1.1 Economic mechanism and research hypotheses

Retail location reflects both demand and supply. Households may choose where to live based on housing prices, jobs and amenities; retailers may locate near customers with purchasing power. A short trip is generally cheaper in time and money, all else equal. Consequently a raw income–distance correlation cannot distinguish consumer preference from residential sorting or retailer location decisions.

H1: Higher-income origins are closer to the two Buckhead destinations. H2: Expected reported flows decline with distance and rise with origin population. H3: Higher-income origins allocate a larger fraction of their reported activity to Buckhead, conditional on distance. H4: A spatial choice model can reproduce future relative shares, and reducing one destination's distance changes its predicted share. These are hypotheses about associations and predictive behavior, not causal identification.

2. Where are the malls relative to income?

This is a spatial association, not evidence that malls cause neighborhood incomes to rise. Adjusted Phase 1 regressions are sensitive to the controls used.

2.1 From raw distances to a retail accessibility index

A nearby large shopping center may offer more opportunities than a nearby small center. Phase 1 therefore explored a potential-accessibility index:

Accessibilityᵢ = Σⱼ wⱼ / (1 + dᵢⱼ)λ

The weight w is an assumed measure of retail opportunities; λ controls how quickly opportunities lose value with distance. Setting λ = 1 or 2 and adding 1 to miles avoids division by zero. These are sensitivity assumptions, not yet estimated behavioral parameters. Equal weighting treats all centers alike; GLA weighting uses leasable floor area; luxury weighting uses store classifications. This is a potential-opportunity index, not a probability or a count of stores actually visited.

The periods are not additive: a device may appear in weekday and weekend data. We use All for the main analysis and analyze the other periods separately. The three destination groups all peak in December. These checks establish file completeness and internal structure, not provider accuracy or coverage of all trips.

3.2 Preliminary seasonal analysis (Phase 3)

Before linking ACS income to CBG mobility, Phase 3 compared the same Georgia origin's reported flows to each Buckhead district relative to Ponce. For positive flows observed at all three destinations in a given origin-month, the outcome was ln(Vᵢⱼₜ / Vᵢ,Ponce,ₜ), regressed on month indicators with standard errors clustered by origin CBG. January is the reference; a coefficient b translates to 100[exp(b) − 1] percent change in the geometric mean flow ratio. This is a selected complete-record comparison, not a measure of total market share. The attempted population crosswalk was not available then; Phase 4 uses an area-based alternative, not the population method.

4. How we combine the data

Dewey reports home locations as census block groups (CBGs), while income and driving distance are organized by ZCTA. We allocate reported counts according to the fraction of each CBG’s area inside each ZCTA.

Weight = overlapping area ÷ total CBG area
Allocated flow = reported CBG count × weight

Summing allocated counts gives 864 observed ZCTA–district–month rows; 828 have income. Area weights assume counts are spread uniformly within each CBG. This is an approximation, not population weighting.

4.1 The actual matching algorithm

Let A(g) be the total polygon area of Georgia origin block group g and A(g ∩ i) the area that overlaps ZCTA i. Both layers are projected into the equal-area EPSG:5070 coordinate system before intersection.

wgi = A(g ∩ i) / A(g)    ;    Vijt = Σg wgi Cgjt

If a CBG reports 100 devices and overlaps ZCTA A by 60% and B by 40%, we allocate 60 and 40. If only 60% overlaps the study region, the remaining 40% is not assigned to study origins. We never normalize those selected overlaps to 100%. The crosswalk contains 755 intersections from 583 block groups. This gives 864 origin–destination–month combinations; excluding missing income leaves 828 (23 × 3 × 12). Fractional values are expected after allocation.

Area weighting assumes uniform spatial distribution within each CBG, which may be false for parks, water and commercial land. Phase 5 therefore changes the assignment rule. Boundary vintages also differ: 2024 Georgia BG and a 2026 ZCTA file with 2020 ZCTA identifiers. Their compatibility with the provider geography is not independently confirmed.

4.2 One real observation

For ZCTA 30313, January, Lenox: recorded road distance 7.7 miles, household median income $84,960, population 10,913 and allocated flow 684.08. This fractional count is an estimate based on geographic overlap, not a fraction of a physical person.

4.3 Who are the origin communities?

Weighted neighborhood incomeⱼ = Σᵢₜ Vᵢⱼₜ Yᵢ / Σᵢₜ Vᵢⱼₜ

This is an activity-weighted average of community median incomes, not the median or mean personal income of shoppers. Phipps' weighted origin income is about 12% above Ponce's in the income-complete study area. This difference could arise from geography, population, retail preferences or provider coverage. Regression helps separate some of these explanations.

5. Gravity model: explaining the volume of flows

A gravity model expects more activity from larger, closer communities. We use Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML), which models a positive expected flow without taking the logarithm of the outcome. The outcome is the allocated reported home-area count.

Expected flow = exp(district effect + month effect + β₁ ln(1 + distance) + β₂ ln(income) + β₃ ln(population))

District and month effects account for persistent differences between destinations and common seasonal changes. The expanded model adds Gini, education, poverty and home values. A separate sorting model uses origin fixed effects and an income × Buckhead interaction to test whether richer origins disproportionately favor Buckhead.

A 10% increase in (1 + distance) is associated with about 9.3% lower predicted flow in the baseline model. Income is not significant at 5%; the sorting interaction is positive (0.228) but uncertain (p = 0.332). Standard errors are clustered by 23 origin ZCTAs.

5.1 Why a gravity model?

Gravity models explain movement between locations using origin mass, destination pull and distance friction. In this setting mass is origin population, pull is a destination effect, and friction is road distance. Retail choices have long been studied using spatial interaction models (Huff, 1963, 1964). Our flow model is an adaptation rather than a replication of a particular paper.

μᵢⱼₜ = exp(αⱼ + δₜ) × (1 + dᵢⱼ)β₁ × Yᵢβ₂ × Nᵢβ₃

μ is the expected allocated flow. α captures persistent destination differences and δ captures common monthly changes. Taking the log of this mean (not the observed outcome) yields the regression equation above. An intercept is necessary because equal distance and population do not imply that different districts have equal activity. Destination effects absorb stable observed and unobserved factors; they cannot tell us which factor is responsible.

5.2 How PPML estimates the model

Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood, implemented as a Poisson generalized linear model with log link, selects coefficients that maximize the following objective up to constants:

ℓ(β) = Σᵢⱼₜ [ Vᵢⱼₜ ln(μᵢⱼₜ) − μᵢⱼₜ ]

The algorithm adjusts parameters using differences between observed and expected flows. PPML does not require a literal Poisson distribution or integer outcomes for consistency under a correctly specified conditional mean and suitable inference. It can retain observed zeros, although missing or suppressed device records are not zeros. Santos Silva and Tenreyro (2006) explain why a log-transformed OLS regression can be problematic with heteroskedastic outcomes. OLS is nevertheless useful as a sensitivity comparison; it estimates a different conditional relationship.

5.3 Elasticities, fixed effects and inference

In a log-link model, changing a logged input from x to rx multiplies expected flow by r raised to its coefficient. Thus a 10% increase in (1 + distance) multiplies flow by 1.10 raised to −1.027 ≈ 0.907, a 9.3% decline. A move from 5 to 6 miles multiplies flow by (7/6) raised to −1.027 ≈ 0.854. The elasticity with respect to actual miles is β₁d/(1+d), not exactly β₁. A 10% population increase predicts approximately 8.95% more flow in the baseline. Income's 0.258 coefficient is positive but not significant at 5%.

A fixed effect is a separate intercept for each group. Destination fixed effects adjust for time-invariant differences across the three districts; month effects adjust for shocks common across destinations. Standard errors are clustered by 23 origin ZCTAs because observations from the same community may have correlated errors. A p-value measures evidence against a zero coefficient under the model assumptions, not the probability a hypothesis is true. With only 23 clusters, normal-based inference can be optimistic; Phase 5 also uses t reference distributions with 22 degrees of freedom.

5.5 Income sorting is a different question

ln(μᵢⱼₜ) = ηᵢ + αⱼ + δₜ + β ln(1 + dᵢⱼ) + θ ln(Yᵢ) Bⱼ

The origin effect η absorbs each community's stable characteristics including its standalone income and population. B = 1 for the two Buckhead destinations and 0 for Ponce. Although income does not vary across destinations for an origin, its interaction with B does. θ measures whether the association between income and relative Buckhead flow is stronger, conditional on distance and fixed effects. The coefficient 0.228 is positive, but p = .332; this is insufficient evidence of additional sorting. It cannot establish preferences for individual luxury brands or personal income effects.

6. Robustness: does the finding survive changes?

Income sorting remains insignificant across the robustness specifications. The November–December income coefficient is larger (0.385, p = 0.024) than January–October (0.227, p = 0.093), but this is exploratory: separate significance results do not establish a significant difference between seasons.

6.1 What changes in each robustness test?

Log-linear OLS regresses ln(V) on the same covariates among positive observations. Weekday and weekend models use separately reported home-area counts. January–October versus November–December models change the time sample. Contained CBGs retains intersections covering at least 99.9% of a CBG; majority-area assignment allocates a CBG entirely to a selected ZCTA only when its overlap exceeds 50% of the total CBG. These tests check geographic allocation assumptions, but they also change coverage and sometimes sample size.

7. Choice model: predicting relative shares

Unlike the gravity model, the calibrated Huff-style model predicts how reported activity from a neighborhood is distributed among the three districts, conditional on these three destinations. It does not predict the total number of shoppers or citywide market share.

Scoreⱼ = district intercept − 1.008 ln(1 + distanceⱼ) + 0.308 × centered ln(income) × Buckheadⱼ
Predicted shareⱼ = exp(scoreⱼ) ÷ sum of exp(scores)

The intercepts capture relative attraction (Lenox 0.411, Phipps 0.140, Ponce 0). Income is centered using the training sample. We fit the parameters to observed shares in January–September (207 origin-month groups) by minimizing cross entropy, then compare predictions with October–December (69 groups). Both models include destination intercepts.

7.1 The classical Huff model and our adaptation

Huff (1963, 1964) proposed a probabilistic retail trade-area model: a large attractive center pulls demand, while distance discourages visits. Let Aⱼ denote an externally specified attractiveness measure (often retail area), a its exponent, and λ the distance penalty.

Pᵢⱼ = [Aⱼa / dᵢⱼλ] / Σₖ [Aₖa / dᵢₖλ]

Taking the logarithm of the unnormalized score yields a ln(Aⱼ) − λ ln(dᵢⱼ). We replace the first term by a fitted destination-specific intercept and use ln(1+d) for finite values at zero distance. We also allow income to change the relative pull of Buckhead destinations. The resulting softmax expression resembles conditional logit (McFadden, 1974), but is fitted to aggregate fractions, not individual choice records.

Uᵢⱼ = αⱼ + β ln(1+dᵢⱼ) + θ[ln(Yᵢ) − c] Bⱼ
Pᵢⱼ = exp(Uᵢⱼ) / Σₖ exp(Uᵢₖ)

7.2 What exactly is attraction?

Only differences between the scores matter; adding the same constant to all three leaves probabilities unchanged. We set Ponce's intercept to zero to identify the other two. In the income-extended model, Lenox's intercept is 0.41084 and Phipps' is 0.13986. Exponentiating gives relative attraction weights of 1.508 and 1.150, versus 1 for Ponce. At equal distance and centered income, these are the predicted flow-share ratios relative to Ponce, not quality ratings or observed sales. The centered log income c = 11.45801 is calculated from the training sample (a geometric-mean income of roughly $94,650). The intercept captures persistent residual differences in destination activities and coverage; it cannot separately identify area, parking, brands or amenities. Only three destinations are available and time-invariant covariates are collinear with destination effects. The earlier directory and GLA weights are not inputs to this final model.

8. Interactive accessibility simulation

What if the driving distance to one district were reduced? Keep income, other distances and model coefficients unchanged, then recompute the share for every neighborhood. This is a hypothetical redistribution, not a causal forecast of a road project.

Shares sum to 100%. Baseline is the income-extended model; all-origin averages match the full-year equal-group weighting because distances and incomes are static.

8.1 Counterfactual mechanics

d′ᵢⱼ = (1−r)dᵢⱼ for improved destination j; other distances unchanged
Δshareⱼ = 100 × meanᵢ[Pᵢⱼ(d′) − Pᵢⱼ(d)]

We hold the fitted coefficients, origin income and other distances fixed. The interactive calculator recomputes all scores and normalizes them again, for the chosen origin or the equal-weight mean of 23 origins. If one destination gains share, at least one other loses share by construction. Example: a 20% reduction for Lenox yields +4.28 percentage points on average, with losses of 1.97 for Phipps and 2.31 for Ponce. This is a conditional simulation, not evidence about what an actual road project will do. The model assumes proportional substitution (the odds between two unchanged alternatives remain fixed), a restrictive property when two malls are close substitutes.

9. Conclusion and limitations

Distance consistently predicts reported mobility, while income provides only limited additional predictive information. This suggests that retail accessibility is a useful way to study geographic differences in shopping opportunities.

Important limitations: only three destinations and 23 income-complete origins; reported home-area counts may be truncated or suppressed; unreported origins are not zero; CBG activity includes non-shopping visits; area allocation is approximate; 2024 and 2026 boundary compatibility has not been independently verified. The ACS income measure is neighborhood-level and five-year, not a direct measure of individual income. These observational results do not establish causal effects of malls or transport changes.

9.1 What the evidence contributes

The project distinguishes geographic access from additional income sorting. Potential access differs across income groups, but after distance is included, the sorting association is uncertain. Across changes to regression and geography, distance retains a strong negative association. A parsimonious structural share formula permits transparent sensitivity exercises but loses to a simple historical benchmark for short-term prediction. Together these are more informative conclusions than a single significant regression.

9.2 Priorities for future research

Verify the provider's geographic vintage, reporting thresholds and licensing; link to mall-specific points of interest if available; obtain same-vintage block-to-ZCTA population weights; expand the sample to many destinations and origins; compare destination area and retail mix using consistent historical definitions; test held-out origins and origin-level spatial dependence. For causal questions about new mall construction, obtain multi-year pre/post outcomes and comparable untreated areas, then assess parallel trends before a difference-in-differences design. None of that causal analysis is claimed here.
